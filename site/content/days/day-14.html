Use today to tell the story of your project. You can keep it private, show it to a trusted adult, or simply write down what you learned.

Tell the story of your project

Start with a simple explanation of what your project helps someone do. Then show a few example questions, the notes it found, and one thing it still gets wrong.

A project is easier to understand when you show what it did and how you checked it. You do not need professional benchmarks or a public website.

A surprising or incorrect result is useful information. It gives you a clue about what to investigate next.

Explain your project in your own words: what you wondered, what you made, what happened, and what you might try next.

What your project needs—and what it does not

For this course, your project can be a small program on your computer that searches a few notes and shows its sources. It does not need a website, a cloud account, a database, or real users.

Professional teams sometimes split large systems into many services. That is an interesting future topic, not a requirement for a good learning project.

For the main course, run it locally with made-up or approved examples. Public deployment is an optional extension that needs a trusted adult to review accounts, costs, data, and safety first.

The build, step by step

  1. Choose one small question your project can help explore.
  2. Try a few example questions and write down which notes your project found.
  3. Use a few made-up or adult-approved notes. Check that no private information is included.
  4. Keep the project on your computer. Public sharing is optional and needs adult review.
  5. Try the same question with two different notes. Did your project find the source you expected?
  6. Ask someone you trust what is clear or confusing, if you want feedback.
  7. Write or draw what the project does, one thing it does well, and one thing it cannot do yet.
  8. Write or draw what worked, what surprised you, and what you are still wondering.

Where people get stuck

  • Changing several things at once. Try one change, then compare again.
  • Using private information or publishing without a trusted adult's review.
  • Thinking that a public demo or account is needed. Your private experiment is enough.
  • Thinking the project must be finished. A small, private experiment is enough.
