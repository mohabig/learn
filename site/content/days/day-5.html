Users don't experience your average latency. They experience the wait before the first word, and you experience the bill at the end of the month. Today you attack both: streaming, two kinds of cache, and a router that only pays for the big model when the small one couldn't do the job.

Streaming, all the way through

Streaming means the provider sends each token as it's produced, instead of holding the whole answer until it's finished.

You pass that on to your own client with server-sent events: one HTTP response that stays open, carrying lines of the form data: ... separated by blank lines, under the content type text/event-stream.

In FastAPI that's an async generator handed to StreamingResponse. In the browser it's EventSource, or a reader on the response body. The FastAPI response docs show the exact shape.

"End to end" is the hard part, and it's where this goes wrong. Every hop in the chain has to refuse to buffer.

Concretely: your handler yields per chunk instead of building up a string, compression is off for that route, and any proxy in front of you is configured not to accumulate the body.

One buffering layer anywhere and your streaming endpoint behaves exactly like a slow ordinary one. It will look perfect on your laptop, because there's no proxy there.

Be honest about what you bought. Streaming does not make anything faster; total time is unchanged. It makes time to first token visible, and that is what people call fast.

The side benefit is real too: long generations stop hitting request timeouts.

So: streaming changes when the first word arrives, not when the last one does — and only if nothing in the chain waits.

Two different caches

There are two caches in play this week, they work differently, and confusing them costs you money.

A response cache is yours. The key is a hash of everything that determines the answer — prompt version, model, parameters, normalized input — and the value is the finished response. A hit costs zero tokens and about a millisecond.

It's only safe when the same key genuinely means the same answer. So use it with temperature near 0, keep personal data out of shared keys, and set a time to live so stale answers expire.

A prompt cache is the provider's, from yesterday. It doesn't need the request to be identical, only the prefix, so it's the one that helps when the document repeats and the question changes.

They compose, and that's the point. The response cache catches exact repeats; the prompt cache catches shared prefixes underneath it.

Measure the hit rate — hits divided by total requests — and log cost with and without. A 30 percent response-cache hit rate takes roughly 30 percent off the bill and drags your median latency toward zero, because a third of your requests never touch a model at all.

So: one cache remembers whole answers, the other remembers the reading you already paid for.

Routing: cheap first, escalate on evidence

Send the request to a small model first. Accept the answer if it passes a check. Escalate to the big model only when it doesn't.

The whole design lives inside that check, so make it mechanical rather than a matter of taste. Did it validate against the schema? Is the required field non-null? Did the citation quote match? Do the numbers add up?

Why does this save money? Think about who answers first. The small model handles most of what arrives, for pennies. The big model only ever sees the questions the small one couldn't handle.

A small model at $0.25 per million and a large one at $3, with 20 percent escalating, gives you an average close to the small model's price and a quality ceiling close to the large one's. That's the deal: the big model's quality, at close to the small model's bill.

Two traps. If you're escalating on almost everything, you pay for both models and add latency on top. So measure the rate — above roughly a third, either improve the small model's prompt or just use the big one.

The second trap is tempting and wrong: don't route on a self-reported confidence score. Models are not calibrated. "confidence: 0.95" is generated text, not a measurement of anything.

So: escalate on something you can check, and only for the requests that actually failed the check.

p50 and p95

Collect the latency of every request in a list and sort it. The value at the halfway point is your median — the name for it is p50. The value 95 percent of the way up is the slowest one in twenty, which is p95.

The average is worse than useless here. Nine requests at 400 ms and one at 9 seconds average out to 1.2 seconds, which describes nobody's experience — while one user in ten waited nine seconds.

Put p95 in your README, because it's the experience of your unluckiest users and it's the number that blows through timeouts.

Measure the categories separately, or you'll be averaging across two different worlds: cached against uncached, small model against escalated, first token against total.

Fifty runs gives you a rough p95. A few hundred makes it stable.

So: report the slowest one in twenty, per category, and never report an average.

The build, step by step

  1. Wrap Day 3's extractor in a FastAPI endpoint. Return a normal JSON response first and confirm it works before adding anything.
  2. Add a streaming route: async generator, StreamingResponse, text/event-stream, one event per chunk, and an explicit end event so the client knows it's done.
  3. Write a client that reads the stream and records the wall-clock time of the first chunk and the last. Never trust a stream you haven't consumed incrementally.
  4. Add the response cache: a dictionary or Redis, keyed on a hash of prompt version, model, parameters, and normalized input, with a time to live. Log hit or miss on every request.
  5. Turn on prompt caching for the stable prefix and log cache-read tokens per request alongside the cost.
  6. Add the router: small model, then a mechanical check, then escalate. Log which path each request took, or you'll never know the escalation rate.
  7. Fire 100 requests — a mix of repeats and new inputs. Compute p50 and p95 for first-token and total time, cached and uncached, and the cost per request for each path.
  8. Put that table in LOG.md. It goes into the README on Day 7 almost unchanged.

Where people get stuck

  • A streaming endpoint that works locally and buffers in production. Test time to first token against the deployed URL, from a different network.
  • Cache keys that include a timestamp or an unsorted dictionary, giving you a 0 percent hit rate that still looks like a working cache.
  • Reporting averages. An average latency and an average cost hide exactly the requests that will generate your support tickets.
  • Routing on the model's own confidence score, which is generated text and not a measurement of anything.
